# Waterloo Region Placemaking Metrics - Definition & Data Reference Guide

**Purpose:** Detailed definitions of key placemaking metrics used in Ontario provincial data collection and reporting on Waterloo Region

**Date:** December 2025

---

## SECTION A: WALKABILITY & PEDESTRIAN ACTIVITY METRICS

### A.1 Walking/Pedestrian Rank (Ministry of Transportation)

**Definition:** 
Ranking of municipalities based on the percentage of daily trips made on foot, derived from the Transportation Tomorrow Survey

**Data Source:** 
Ontario Ministry of Transportation, Transportation Tomorrow Survey

**Coverage:** 
33 Ontario municipalities

**Measurement Period:** 
Annual (2024 most recent release)

**Waterloo Region Results:**
- **City of Waterloo:** #2 in Ontario (only behind Toronto)
- **City of Kitchener:** #25
- **City of Cambridge:** #27

**What It Measures:**
- Appeal and accessibility of walking as transportation mode
- Urban design quality supporting pedestrian movement
- Infrastructure quality (sidewalks, lighting, safety)
- Urban density and service proximity
- Weather and climate considerations (sidewalk maintenance)

**Provincial Context:**
- #1: Toronto (~20% of trips on foot)
- #2: Waterloo (~18-20% estimated)
- #3-33: Other Ontario communities

**Relevance to Placemaking:**
Indicates successful creation of walkable, mixed-use communities where residents can meet daily needs on foot

**Data Access:**
- https://kitchener.citynews.ca/2025/01/14/waterloo-likes-to-walk-while-cambridge-kitchener-lag-behind/
- Annual releases from Ministry

---

### A.2 Weekly Walking Trips Count

**Definition:** 
Total number of walking trips completed by residents in a community during a typical week

**Data Source:** 
Ontario Ministry of Transportation, Transportation Tomorrow Survey

**Waterloo Region Data:**
- **City of Waterloo:** 47,710 trips per week (2024)
- **Comparison:** Higher than Kitchener and Cambridge

**Calculation:** 
Survey-based extrapolation from representative sample

**What It Indicates:**
- Absolute walking activity level
- Size-adjusted measure (population matters)
- Community walkability in real-world terms
- Cumulative benefit of pedestrian infrastructure

**Provincial Significance:**
Toronto higher in absolute terms; Waterloo among highest in per-capita terms

**Interpretation for Placemaking:**
Higher walking trip counts indicate:
- Successful pedestrian infrastructure investment
- Mixed-use urban development
- Compact neighborhood design
- Service accessibility within walking distance

---

### A.3 Walkability Assessment (High/Very High Walkable Areas)

**Definition:** 
Percentage of population living in areas designated as "high" or "very high" walkability based on urban design metrics

**Data Source:** 
Region of Waterloo CTC Monitoring Program; based on urban design analysis

**Metric Type:** 
Geographic/demographic intersection

**What It Measures:**
- Population access to walkable neighborhoods
- Distribution of high-quality pedestrian infrastructure
- Equity of pedestrian amenity access
- Correlation with MTSA (Major Transit Station Area) proximity

**Components of Walkability Assessment:**
- Pedestrian network connectivity
- Mixed-use development density
- Street network design (block size, intersection density)
- Sidewalk completeness
- Lighting and safety features
- Weather protection elements
- Traffic speed and volume
- Intersection design (pedestrian crossing quality)

**Status in CTC:** 
2024 report indicates metric pending completion

**Relevance to Placemaking:**
Core metric for assessing success of transit-oriented development and downtown revitalization

**Related Concept:** "Walkability Index" - commonly uses metrics like:
- Pedestrian friendliness score (0-100)
- Walking distance to transit (target: 400m-800m)
- Block size (optimal: 80-120m)
- Sidewalk continuity (% complete)

## SECTION B: TRANSIT & ACTIVE TRANSPORTATION METRICS

### B.1 Transit Mode Share

**Definition:** 
Percentage of all daily trips that utilize public transportation (Grand River Transit, ION light rail, GO Transit, etc.)

**Data Source:** 
Ontario Ministry of Transportation, Transportation Tomorrow Survey; Grand River Transit operational data

**Historical Data:**
- **2011 Baseline:** 5.3% of Waterloo Region trips used Grand River Transit
- **Expected Growth:** Post-ION implementation (2019-2024)
- **2024 Estimated:** Higher percentage (specific data pending 2024 report)

**Calculation Method:**
Total transit trips / Total daily trips × 100

**Provincial Context:**
- Toronto: ~20-25% transit mode share
- GTA averages: 10-15%
- Suburban areas: 5-10%

**What It Indicates:**
- Success of transit system design and accessibility
- Integration of transit with urban land use
- Community adoption of sustainable transportation
- Quality and reliability of transit service

**Waterloo Region Factors Driving Growth:**
- ION light rail opening (2019)
- MTSA densification
- Parking policies
- Employer transit incentives
- Student population demand (University of Waterloo)

**Relevance to Placemaking:**
High transit mode share enables:
- Walkable neighborhoods without car dependence
- Reduced vehicle emissions
- More public space (less parking)
- Community gathering opportunities

---

### B.2 Active Transportation Mode Share

**Definition:** 
Percentage of daily trips made by walking or cycling combined

**Data Source:** 
Ontario Ministry of Transportation, Transportation Tomorrow Survey

**Components:**
- Walking trips (pedestrian)
- Cycling trips (bicycle)

**Waterloo Region Status:**
- Growing trend since 2011
- More pronounced in areas with dedicated cycling infrastructure
- City of Waterloo leading among Waterloo Region municipalities

**Provincial Significance:**
Contributes to overall sustainable transportation goal (walking + cycling + transit ideally 30%+)

**What It Measures:**
- Effectiveness of active transportation infrastructure
- Community support for sustainable modes
- Public health benefits (physical activity)
- Environmental sustainability (zero emissions)

**Relevance to Placemaking:**
Robust active transportation enables:
- "15-minute neighborhoods" (daily needs within 15-min walk/cycle)
- Complete streets design
- Reduced traffic congestion
- Enhanced public safety through "eyes on street"

---

### B.3 GHG Emissions per Capita

**Definition:** 
Tonnes of net air emissions (greenhouse gases) per capita per year in a community

**Data Source:** 
Region of Waterloo CTC Monitoring; Environment Canada data; transportation emission models

**Geography:** 
Tracked for Cambridge, Kitchener, and Waterloo combined (CTC area)

**Relationship to Transportation:**
- Transportation (~30-40% of urban emissions)
- Building energy use
- Waste management
- Other sources

**Measurement Period:** 
Annual

**Status:** 
Actively tracked; year-over-year comparison

**What It Indicates:**
- Effectiveness of emissions reduction policies
- Impact of transit investment and mode shift
- Success of climate action planning
- Need for additional interventions

**Provincial Targets:**
Ontario committed to 50% emissions reduction by 2030 (2005 baseline)

**Relevance to Placemaking:**
Demonstrates that placemaking initiatives (compact development, transit, active transportation) contribute to climate goals

**Related Metrics:**
- Tonnes CO2eq (Carbon dioxide equivalent)
- Per capita vs. per household vs. per unit GDP
- Sectoral breakdown (transportation, buildings, etc.)

## SECTION C: DENSITY & BUILT ENVIRONMENT METRICS

### C.1 MTSA (Major Transit Station Area) Density

**Definition:** 
Population and/or employment density achieved within defined areas surrounding transit stations (typically 400-800m radius)

**Data Source:** 
Region of Waterloo planning department; census data; building permit data

**MTSA Definition:**
- Circular or polygon area around ION light rail stations
- Standard radius: 800m (¼ mile "pedestrian shed")
- Inclusive of direct station access + surrounding neighborhood

**Measurement Units:**
- Persons per hectare (common in Canada)
- Alternative: Dwelling units per hectare; Floor Area Ratio (FAR)

**Provincial Target (Growth Plan):**
- Minimum 50 residents/jobs per hectare in most MTSAs
- Higher targets (100+) in major urban centers

**Waterloo Region MTSAs:**
- Stage 1: Waterloo to Kitchener (9 stations)
- Stage 2: Kitchener to Cambridge (anticipated)

**Tracking:**
CTC Monitoring Program: "Density achieved at each of the Region's MTSAs" indicator

**What It Measures:**
- Successful transit-oriented development (TOD)
- Mixed-use intensification
- Walkability potential
- Transit ridership capacity

**2011 Baseline:** ION approved; density tracking begins

**Relevance to Placemaking:**
Higher MTSA density enables:
- Pedestrian-oriented retail and services
- Diverse housing supply
- Vibrant public spaces
- Efficient use of infrastructure

---

### C.2 Land Use Mix

**Definition:** 
Percentage of regional land uses within the Central Transit Corridor compared to total regional land uses

**Components Measured:**
- Residential (various densities)
- Commercial (retail, office)
- Industrial/Employment
- Institutional (schools, hospitals, government)
- Parks and open space
- Transportation infrastructure

**Data Source:** 
Region of Waterloo land use mapping; municipal zoning data; property assessment data

**Indicator Formula:**
(Land uses in CTC) / (Total land uses in Region) × 100

**Measurement Frequency:** 
Annual review

**Status in CTC:** 
Actively tracked; 2024 data available

**What It Measures:**
- Concentration of mixed-use development
- Effectiveness of growth center strategy
- Diversity of housing, jobs, services in defined corridors
- Deviation from single-use zoning patterns

**Significance:**
Higher land use mix correlates with:
- Reduced vehicle travel distances
- More walkable neighborhoods
- Economic vitality
- Community vibrancy

**Relevance to Placemaking:**
Mixed-use development creates:
- "Eyes on the street" (Jane Jacobs principle)
- Pedestrian activation
- Economic resilience
- Social interaction spaces

## SECTION D: CULTURAL & VIBRANCY METRICS

### D.1 Arts & Culture Establishments Count

**Definition:** 
Number of arts and culture establishments within the Central Transit Corridor (and Region overall)

**Categories Include:**
- Art galleries and museums
- Performance venues (theaters, concert halls)
- Artist studios
- Cultural centers
- Heritage sites and museums
- Creative industries offices
- Arts-focused restaurants and cafes

**Data Source:** 
CTC Monitoring Program; business registries; census data on cultural occupation

**Measurement Method:** 
Count-based; geographic intersection with GIS

**Status in CTC Monitoring:** 
2024 report indicates data pending completion

**Why It's Measured:**
- Indicates cultural vitality and placemaking success
- Attracts younger, educated workforce (talent retention)
- Generates foot traffic and vibrancy
- Supports local economy and entrepreneurship
- Contributes to unique place identity

**Relevance to Placemaking:**
Arts and culture establishments are cornerstone of:
- Downtown revitalization
- Innovation districts (e.g., Kitchener)
- Community identity and pride
- Attraction of talent and investment

**Related Concept:** "Cultural Vibrancy" - broader measure including:
- Event attendance
- Public art installations
- Busking and street performance
- Community participation in arts

**Waterloo Region Context:**
- Kitchener: Arts/culture-led revitalization (THEMUSEUM, festivals)
- Waterloo: University-linked cultural activities
- Cambridge: Heritage-based attractions

---

### D.2 Restaurants as Community Anchor

**Definition:** 
Percentage of region's restaurants located within the Central Transit Corridor

**Data Source:** 
Business registries; municipal licensing data; business directories

**Metric Formula:**
(Restaurants in CTC) / (Total restaurants in Region) × 100

**Waterloo Region Data:**
Actively tracked in CTC Monitoring (2024 data available)

**What It Indicates:**
- Economic vitality and investment concentration
- Pedestrian attraction and street activation
- Neighborhood character and diversity
- Commercial success of downtown areas

**Significance for Placemaking:**
Restaurants as "third places" enable:
- Casual social gathering
- Neighborhood socialization
- Economic clustering (food clusters)
- Extended retail hours (foot traffic at night)
- Weather-protected public engagement

**Types Considered:**
- Fine dining
- Casual dining
- Cafes and coffee shops
- Food courts
- Quick service restaurants
- Specialty cuisines
- Delivery-focused establishments (increasingly)

## SECTION E: HERITAGE & SUSTAINABILITY METRICS

### E.1 Heritage Resource Retention

**Definition:** 
Number (or trend) of demolition permits issued for pre-1920 or designated heritage buildings

**Data Source:** 
CTC Monitoring; municipal building permit records; heritage inventory

**Methodology:**
Count of demolition permits → indicator of heritage loss
Lower numbers = better retention

**Context:**
- Pre-1920 buildings: Often highest heritage/cultural value
- Designated heritage properties: Legal protection but demolition still possible
- Adaptive reuse: Preferred alternative to demolition

**Measurement Period:** 
Annual count; trend analysis over 5-10 years

**Status in CTC:** 
Actively tracked; 2024 data available

**Waterloo Region Heritage Examples:**
- Breithaupt Block (Kitchener) - heritage adaptive reuse
- The Tannery (Kitchener) - heritage adaptive reuse
- Simpson Block (Kitchener) - heritage conservation
- Walper Hotel (Kitchener) - heritage restoration

**Relevance to Placemaking:**
Heritage preservation supports:
- Unique place identity
- Economic resilience (heritage tourism)
- Cultural continuity and pride
- Sustainable "new" development (adaptive reuse vs. demolition)
- Walkable, human-scale streetscapes (pre-1920 era walkable neighborhoods)

**Policy Tools:**
- Heritage designation
- Demolition delays and review processes
- Heritage tax credits
- Adaptive reuse incentives
- Heritage area overlay zoning

## SECTION F: INVESTMENT & ECONOMIC METRICS

### F.1 Building Permit Value

**Definition:** 
Total dollar value of building permits issued for new construction within the Central Transit Corridor

**Data Source:** 
Municipal building permit records; assessment authority data

**Measurement:** 
Annual dollar amount (millions)

**Waterloo Region Data:**
- 2022: Record year - $1.8 billion total regional permits
- CTC specific: Data available in CTC Monitoring

**Calculation:**
Sum of all construction values permitted in given year

**What It Measures:**
- Private sector confidence in area
- Investment concentration
- Development momentum
- Economic vitality signals

**Related Metric:** Building Permit Volume (number of permits)

**Significance:**
- Higher values indicate successful placemaking attraction
- Concentration in CTC validates growth center strategy
- Year-over-year trends show momentum
- Sectoral breakdown reveals housing vs. commercial vs. industrial focus

**Waterloo Region Trend:**
- Consistent growth in CTC building investment
- 2022 increase: +6% over 2021
- Outpacing 10-year average by 33%

**Relevance to Placemaking:**
Building investment demonstrates:
- Market confidence in placemaking initiatives
- Readiness for population growth
- Private sector alignment with public planning
- Vitality and attractiveness of public realm

---

### F.2 Assessment/Property Value

**Definition:** 
Total assessed property value of properties within the Central Transit Corridor

**Data Source:** 
Municipal property assessment authority; assessment rolls

**Measurement:** 
Aggregate assessed value (billions); per-property average

**What It Measures:**
- Wealth concentration
- Land value premium from proximity to transit
- Long-term investment returns
- Tax base growth

**Significance:**
- Increasing assessment values = successful TOD
- Premium pricing near transit validates walkability
- Tax revenue growth enables municipal investment
- Investment comparison (CTC vs. rest of region)

**Equity Considerations:**
- Rising property values = gentrification risk
- Affordability pressure as assessment increases
- Importance of community housing policies

**Relevance to Placemaking:**
Property value appreciation indicates:
- Successful creation of desirable places
- Market validation of planning decisions
- Need for affordability safeguards
- Sustainability of development economics

## SECTION G: SAFETY & PERCEPTION METRICS

### G.1 Safety Perception

**Definition:** 
Percentage of residents who perceive their downtown area as safe at night

**Data Source:** 
Community surveys; opinion research; CTC Monitoring Program

**Measurement Method:** 
Random sample survey; perception question on nighttime safety
Typical question: "Do you feel safe walking in downtown [city] after dark?"

**Status in CTC:** 
2024 report indicates data pending completion

**What It Indicates:**
- Subjective sense of security
- Actual crime prevention effectiveness
- Quality of lighting and public realm design
- Community policing success
- Vibrancy and "eyes on the street" (activity level)

**Relationship to Actual Crime:**
- Perception ≠ actual crime rates
- Design elements: street lighting, sightlines, enclosures
- Activity level: more people = perceived safety
- Maintenance level: neglected areas perceived as unsafe

**Importance for Placemaking:**
- Residents won't use spaces they perceive as unsafe
- Nighttime activation requires confidence
- Affects restaurant/entertainment district vitality
- Critical for women, elderly, youth comfort

**Design Interventions:**
- Improve lighting
- Remove sight obstructions
- Add seating and gathering spaces (more people)
- Maintenance and cleanliness
- Visible security presence
- Active ground-floor uses

---

### G.2 Police Calls for Service (Public Perception Related)

**Definition:** 
Percentage of police calls for service in downtown area related to activities affecting public perception (vs. actual crime)

**Data Source:** 
Police service records; call categorization

**Categories Typically Included:**
- Noise complaints
- Loitering concerns
- Nuisance complaints
- Quality of life issues
- Intoxication in public spaces
- Panhandling concerns
- Graffiti/property appearance

**Measurement:**
(Perception-related calls) / (Total calls in area) × 100

**Status in CTC:** 
Actively tracked; 2024 data available

**What It Indicates:**
- "Broken windows" effect (maintenance, minor issues)
- Community comfort level
- Unmet social/health needs in area
- Effectiveness of public order enforcement
- Vibrancy vs. abandonment perception

**Relevance to Placemaking:**
Managing perception-related calls requires:
- Proactive maintenance
- Social services integration
- Activity programming
- Community engagement
- Balanced enforcement approach

## SECTION H: INCLUSIVITY & AFFORDABILITY METRICS

### H.1 Housing Affordability (Home Ownership Transactions)

**Definition:** 
Percentage of housing transactions within the Central Transit Corridor that are affordable to low- and moderate-income (LMI) households

**Data Source:** 
Real estate transaction records; mortgage data; income data

**Affordability Definition:**
- Typically: Housing cost ≤ 30% of household gross income
- LMI: Households earning up to 60-80% of area median income (AMI)

**Measurement:**
(Affordable transactions) / (Total transactions in CTC) × 100

**Waterloo Region Context:**
- Growing concern as property values increase
- Gentrification risk in revitalized areas
- Policy focus on inclusionary zoning

**Status in CTC:** 
Actively tracked; 2024 data available

**What It Indicates:**
- Inclusivity of growth benefits
- Wealth disparity trends
- Success of affordability policies
- Risk of displacement

**Relevance to Placemaking:**
Successful placemaking requires:
- Economic diversity maintained
- Multigenerational families staying
- Service workers able to live near jobs
- Community stability
- Reduced commute distances

**Policy Tools:**
- Community Housing (non-market)
- Rent control measures
- Inclusionary zoning (% affordable in new development)
- Property tax relief for LMI owners
- Tenant protections

---

### H.2 Community Housing Supply

**Definition:** 
Number of Community Housing units located within the Central Transit Corridor

**Data Source:** 
Housing Authority records; municipal housing databases

**Community Housing Definition (Ontario):**
- Non-market housing
- Rent-geared-to-income (RGI) basis
- Accessibility-supportive
- Other non-profit housing

**Measurement:** 
Count of units; tracking over time

**Waterloo Region Context:**
- Region has CityHousing Waterloo Region (public provider)
- Community housing sits within regional strategy
- Limited supply relative to need

**Status in CTC:** 
Actively tracked; 2024 data available

**What It Measures:**
- Inclusivity of downtown
- Accessibility for vulnerable populations
- Diversity of housing options
- Public sector housing commitment

**Relevance to Placemaking:**
Community Housing in CTCs enables:
- Seniors aging in place (transit-accessible location)
- Families without private car ownership
- Stability and neighborhood roots
- Mixed-income communities
- Service provider proximity (health, transit, retail)

## SECTION I: VISION 1 MILLION SCORECARD METRICS

Composite assessment of place quality, community vibrancy, and livability in Waterloo Region

**Sub-Dimensions:** 
- Arts and culture vitality
- Public space quality
- Community gathering spaces
- Walkability infrastructure
- Heritage and cultural preservation
- Social inclusion and community engagement
- Affordable amenity access
- Neighborhood character

- Multiple data sources integrated
- Qualitative and quantitative elements
- Comparative assessment (region readiness for 1M population)
- Trend analysis (improving/declining)

**Score System:**
- Status-based categories (e.g., "On Track," "Needs Acceleration," "Lagging," "Enhanced")
- Regular updates and version releases

**Version 1 Status (January 2026):**
- Framework established
- Metrics refined
- Baseline data collected
- Annual tracking initiated

- Overall community quality of life
- Attractiveness to residents and businesses

## SECTION J: DATA COLLECTION METHODS & LIMITATIONS

### J.1 Survey-Based Metrics

**Examples:** Walking trips, transit mode share, safety perception, household income

**Method:** Random sample surveys (phone, online, in-person)
**Frequency:** Annual (Transportation Tomorrow Survey)
**Sample Size:** Representative sample (100s to 1000s)
**Confidence:** 95% typical confidence level; 5% margin of error

**Limitations:**
- Response bias (who responds to surveys)
- Seasonal variation not always captured
- Self-reported data may have inaccuracy
- Cost and resource intensity
- Privacy concerns

---

### J.2 Administrative Data

**Examples:** Building permits, business licenses, real estate transactions, property assessment

**Method:** Compiled from municipal records, registries, assessment authority
**Frequency:** Continuous/annual
**Coverage:** Complete universe (all permits, all transactions)
**Reliability:** High (official records)

**Limitations:**
- May lag data collection
- Categorization varies by municipality
- Privacy restrictions on some data
- Historical consistency issues

---

### J.3 GIS & Mapping Methods

**Examples:** Population density, land use mix, MTSA proximity, walkability network

**Method:** Geographic information system analysis
**Data Sources:** Municipal zoning, property parcels, census data, infrastructure mapping
**Frequency:** Annual or as updated
**Precision:** Granular (property-level or smaller)

**Limitations:**
- GIS data quality depends on municipal investment
- Categorization choices affect results
- Time-consuming to update
- Requires technical expertise

---

### J.4 Census & Official Statistics

**Examples:** Population, employment, household composition, income

**Method:** National census (5-year cycle); annual estimates
**Coverage:** Complete enumeration (every household)
**Reliability:** High official statistics

**Limitations:**
- Long collection lag (census data 2+ years old at release)
- Small area data limited
- Privacy restrictions
- Costs limit frequency

## SECTION K: INTERPRETING PLACEMAKING METRICS

### K.1 Holistic Assessment

**Key Principle:** No single metric tells full story
- Walking rank = #2 (strong) but density still developing
- Building permits rising (good) but affordability declining (concerning)
- Heritage retention good but cultural vibrancy data pending

**Integrated Analysis:**
- Trends over time (trajectory)
- Comparison to peers (ranking)
- Benchmarking to targets (adequacy)
- Equity across neighborhoods (distribution)

### K.2 Leading vs. Lagging Indicators

**Leading Indicators** (predictive of future performance):
- Building permits (projects coming)
- Investment announcements (economic confidence)
- Population density growth (walkability potential)
- Business formation (economic vitality)

**Lagging Indicators** (reflect past actions):
- Crime statistics
- Safety perception
- Heritage preservation
- Community health outcomes

### K.3 Context-Specific Interpretation

**Waterloo Region Context:**
- Major growth region (different metrics than stable region)
- University presence (affects demographics, walkability)
- Tech sector concentration (jobs, income, talent)
- Cold climate winters (affects walking, cycling)
- Rapid infrastructure development (ongoing transition)
